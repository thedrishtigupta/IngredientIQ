# Research Study: Do Ingredients Predict Product Success?

This document covers the research track of the synopsis (section 2.6): what was built, every methodological decision and why it was made, how to reproduce the results, and what the results mean.

The numbers themselves are in [RESEARCH_RESULTS.md](RESEARCH_RESULTS.md), which is generated by the pipeline and must not be edited by hand. The same results are shown on the `/research` page of the web app.

## 1. Research Question

> Do individual ingredients and ingredient combinations carry statistically and predictively meaningful information about a cosmetic product's success, over and above what is already explained by brand, category, price and existing popularity?

The study answers this in two complementary ways:

1. **Statistical association (step 3).** Is the presence of an ingredient or combination associated with better outcomes, and does that still hold when brand, subcategory and price are held fixed?
2. **Incremental prediction (step 5).** Does a model given ingredient features (Model B) predict success better than the same model without them (Model A)?

SHAP (step 6) then shows what the ingredient-aware model relies on, and cross-checks that against step 1.

All findings are associations in this dataset. None of them is a causal, medical or safety claim.

## 2. How to Run

```powershell
pip install -r requirements.txt
python scripts/run_research.py                  # full run, about 1 hour (mostly step 5)
python scripts/run_research.py --quick          # 1 CV repeat, LightGBM only: about 7 minutes
python scripts/run_research.py --reuse-models   # redo everything except step 5's cross-validation
pytest tests/test_research.py
```

The pipeline reads only `data/raw/product_info.csv` and the processed CSVs in `data/processed/`. It needs no database and no LLM, and all randomness is seeded (`SEED = 42`). The same inputs therefore always give the same results.

| Output | Content |
|---|---|
| `data/research/products.parquet` | The analysis table: one row per product, all targets and features |
| `data/research/combinations.csv` | Mined ingredient combinations (support, lift, category) |
| `data/research/associations.csv` | Every association test: raw and adjusted effects, p and q values |
| `data/research/functional_groups.csv` | Kruskal–Wallis tests for functional-group shares |
| `data/research/cv_folds.csv` | Every fold score of every model (the raw material of step 5) |

## 4. Data and Scope (Steps 1–2)

### 4.1 Which products are analysed

| Stage | Products |
|---|---:|
| Catalogue | 8,494 |
| In scope | 6,320 |
| **After keeping one product per family** | **5,547** |

Reasons a catalogue product is out of scope (a product can have more than one):

- no ingredient list (950 products);
- a multi-component kit with several formulas in one listing (713 products);
- a gift set, mini or tool, by primary or secondary category.

**Why deduplicate.** A mini and its full-size version have the same formula. If both were kept, the same formula could land in the training fold and the test fold, and the ingredient model would partly be scored on products it had already seen. Products are grouped into a family when they share either:

- the same brand and the same name once size words are removed ("Mini", "Travel Size", "1 oz"…), or
- the same brand and an identical ingredient list.

Groups are merged transitively (union-find). The family member with the most reviews is kept.

### 4.2 Ingredients

- Only `PRIMARY` ingredients are used. `May Contain` sections (shade-dependent colourants) are excluded, because a product does not necessarily contain them.
- Spellings are merged: "Aqua/Water/Eau", "Water" and "Water/Aqua/Eau" become `water`, and "Parfum (Fragrance)" becomes `fragrance`. Parser artefacts such as `1` or `*` are dropped.
- **Prominence** is a proxy for concentration (labels list ingredients in descending concentration above 1%). The first ingredient scores 1 and the last scores close to 0.

### 4.3 Success indicators (targets)

| Target | Definition | Products |
|---|---|---:|
| `rating` | Catalogue average rating, **only if ≥ 25 reviews** | 4,617 |
| `log_loves` | log(1 + loves count) | 5,547 |
| `log_reviews` | log(1 + review count) | 5,547 |
| `rec_rate` | Share of reviewers who recommend, **only if ≥ 25 votes** | 1,529 |
| `high_performer` | Top 25% within its primary category on a composite score (below) | 5,547 |

- **Rating threshold.** A 5.0 from 2 reviews is noise, not success. The threshold keeps 4,617 products.
- **Recommendation rate exists only for Skincare.** The review files cover Skincare products only, so this target is analysed in the Skincare scope alone.
- **Composite score.** Within its primary category, each product is ranked on three indicators, and the three percentile ranks are averaged:
  - a *shrunk* rating, pulled toward the category mean when a product has few reviews;
  - log loves;
  - log reviews.

  Ranking within the category stops the label from simply meaning "is Skincare" or "is Fragrance".
- **Correlations.** Rating and recommendation rate are almost the same measure (Spearman ρ = 0.94). Rating and loves are slightly *negatively* related (ρ = −0.15): popular products are not the best rated. This is why each indicator is modelled separately.

### 4.4 Features

**Model A (baseline, no ingredient information)**
- Categorical: brand, primary, secondary and tertiary category. Brands with fewer than 10 products share one "rare" column.
- Numeric: log price, value-price flag, on-sale flag, limited edition, new, online only, out of stock, Sephora exclusive, child count, has variations.
- Existing popularity is included where it is not circular:

  | Target | Popularity features in Model A |
  |---|---|
  | `rating`, `rec_rate` | log loves, log reviews |
  | `log_loves`, `log_reviews` | none: loves and reviews are near copies of each other, so predicting one from the other is not a test of anything |
  | `high_performer` | none: the label is built from those indicators |

**Model B = Model A plus**
- Presence of the 300 most common ingredients.
- Prominence of the 100 most common.
- Number of ingredients.
- Share of the formula in each of 37 functional groups (humectant, emollient, UV filter…), from the ingredient knowledge layer.
- 134 combination flags (section 5).

That is 572 ingredient features in total.

## 5. Combinations (Step 4)

FP-Growth (`mlxtend`) runs **separately in each category** with these settings:

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Minimum support | 5% of the category's products |
| Itemset size | pairs and triples |
| Minimum lift | 1.5 |

- **Why lift.** Combinations that occur together only by chance (lift ≈ 1, e.g. two ubiquitous preservatives) are not combinations in any meaningful sense, so they are dropped.
- **Why a quota.** At most 30 combinations are kept per category. Without the quota, Fragrance's allergen clusters (linalool + citral + geraniol…) filled the entire list.
- **Leakage.** Mining never sees a target, so running it on the full dataset does not leak outcome information into the models.

**Pre-specified hypotheses.** The synopsis names glycerin + hyaluronic acid and niacinamide + zinc. They are always tested and reported separately as confirmatory, whatever their lift.

- Both forms of hyaluronic acid on labels are tested: `sodium hyaluronate` and `hyaluronic acid`.
- **Zinc PCA is too rare in this catalogue** to have a feature column, so niacinamide + zinc cannot be tested. The report says so.

## 6. Statistical Analysis (Step 3)

Tests run in seven scopes: all products, Skincare, Makeup, Hair, Fragrance, and Bath & Body (Men, with 54 products, is too small). Each scope is crossed with each target and with every feature that at least 30 products have and at least 30 lack.

| Question | Continuous target | High-performer label |
|---|---|---|
| Raw difference with vs without | Mann–Whitney U, **Cliff's δ** (also Cohen's d) | Chi-square, **Cramér's V** (and rate difference) |
| Does a higher list position matter? | Spearman ρ between prominence and outcome, among products containing the ingredient | — |
| Functional groups (none / low / high share) | Kruskal–Wallis, **ε²**, plus Spearman ρ | Same |
| **Adjusted** difference | Regression with brand, subcategory and log price as controls; effect in standard deviations of the outcome | Same (linear probability model) |

**Why the adjusted test.** This is the study's main addition to the synopsis. A raw association is easily produced by brand: if one popular brand puts an ingredient in most of its products, that ingredient looks "successful". The adjusted test compares products of the same brand and subcategory at the same price.

- It uses the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem: the controls are removed once from the outcome and from all features. This gives exactly the per-feature regression coefficient, but hundreds of times faster.
- `tests/test_research.py::test_adjustment_removes_a_pure_brand_effect` checks that a purely brand-driven ingredient comes out near zero while a real effect survives.

**Multiple comparisons.** Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction is applied within each scope × target family, separately to the raw and the adjusted p-values. The corrected value is called *q*.

**Robust association** = raw q < 0.05 **and** adjusted q < 0.05 **and** the same sign. Only robust associations are presented as findings.

## 7. Predictive Modelling (Steps 5–6)

### 7.1 Models and comparison

| Task | Interpretable baseline | Tree ensembles |
|---|---|---|
| Regression (rating, loves, reviews, recommendation rate) | RidgeCV (regularisation tuned by inner CV) | Random Forest, LightGBM |
| Classification (high performer) | LogisticRegressionCV (tuned, class-balanced) | Random Forest, LightGBM (class-balanced) |

Every model is trained as Model A and as Model B **on identical folds**, so the feature set is the only difference.

**Why the linear models are tuned.** A first run with a fixed Ridge penalty made Model B *worse*: with 572 extra columns, the same penalty under-regularises. That reflects a tuning artefact, not missing signal, so each variant now chooses its own penalty.

### 7.2 Two validation schemes

| Scheme | What it does | What it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **random** | 5-fold cross-validation, repeated 3 times (stratified for classification) | Does B predict better? |
| **unseen_brand** | 5 folds that each hold out whole brands | Can ingredients predict success for brands the model has never seen? |

Unseen-brand is the stricter test. It stops the model from leaning on "brand X is popular", which is exactly where ingredient information should help if it carries real signal.

### 7.3 Significance

The synopsis proposes a paired t-test on fold scores. Folds share most of their training data, so that test is too optimistic: it finds "significant" differences too easily. The verdict therefore uses the **Nadeau–Bengio corrected resampled t-test**, which widens the variance by a factor of (1/k + n_test/n_train). The plain paired p-value is still stored in `model_comparison.csv`.

Primary metrics are R² for regression and ROC-AUC for classification. All synopsis metrics are also reported: MAE, RMSE, accuracy, precision, recall and F1.

### 7.4 SHAP

1. A LightGBM Model B is trained on all products of the scope.
2. `shap.TreeExplainer` attributes every prediction to every feature.
3. Global importance is the mean absolute SHAP value. Direction is the sign of the correlation between a feature's value and its SHAP value.
4. Importance is summed per feature family (baseline, ingredient, functional group, combination).
5. **Cross-check:** every ingredient or combination in the top list is looked up in the step 3 table. It counts as *confirmed* only if it is a robust association there, in the same direction.

## 8. Deviations from the Synopsis

| Synopsis | What was done | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ~499 reviewed products, 119k reviews | 1,529 products with a reliable recommendation rate (from 1.09M reviews) | The processed review data is larger than when the synopsis was written; the ≥ 25-vote rule and deduplication reduce it to 1,529 |
| Correlation, t-test/ANOVA, chi-square, BH, effect sizes | All of these (non-parametric versions where outcomes are skewed) **plus** a brand/subcategory/price-adjusted test | Raw tests are dominated by brand confounding (section 9) |
| Paired t-test on folds | Nadeau–Bengio corrected t-test (plain one also stored) | Fold scores are not independent |
| Model A includes "existing popularity" | Included only for rating and recommendation rate | Circular for the loves and review-count targets |
| XGBoost/LightGBM | LightGBM only | Equivalent role; one gradient-boosting library keeps the run time down |
| Niacinamide + zinc combination | Not testable | Zinc PCA is too rare in this catalogue |
| — | Unseen-brand validation, product-family deduplication, within-category combination mining | Needed for the conclusions to be trustworthy |

## 9. Findings

From the full run. Exact numbers are in [RESEARCH_RESULTS.md](RESEARCH_RESULTS.md); the robustness checks are in [RESEARCH_ROBUSTNESS.md](RESEARCH_ROBUSTNESS.md).

**1. Most raw ingredient–success associations are brand and category effects.**

| Stage | Associations |
|---|---:|
| Tests run (scope × target × feature) | 6,117 |
| Significant before adjustment (q < 0.05) | 1,608 |
| Still significant with brand, subcategory and price held fixed | 187 |
| Robust (both tests, same sign) | 127 |

The collapse is starkest in Fragrance: dozens of raw hits per target, and at most one survives adjustment. Fragrance allergens (linalool, citral…) mark *which kind* of fragrance a product is, not whether it succeeds. The robust associations that remain are modest, about 0.2–0.5 SD of the outcome. Many involve formulation-base or texture ingredients (film formers, silicones, emulsifiers) rather than marketed actives.

**2. Ingredient features add no predictive value.**
- Across 54 Model A vs Model B comparisons (2 scopes × targets × 3 models × 2 validation schemes), Model B is **never significantly better**.
- It is **significantly worse in 21**, all of them linear or random-forest models. Those models degrade when 572 mostly sparse, uninformative columns are added: ridge applies one penalty to every column, and the random forest's feature subsampling dilutes the strong brand and category splits.
- LightGBM handles wide sparse inputs best. Its largest gain is +0.040 R² (Skincare rating, unseen brands), and none of its gains is significant.

**3. Attribution is not added value.**
- The same LightGBM Model B attributes **43–69% of its SHAP importance** to ingredient-derived features, while gaining nothing in accuracy.
- The ingredient features largely re-encode brand and category: brands reuse bases, preservative systems and fragrance across their ranges. A model given both can split on either.
- A SHAP chart read on its own would therefore suggest that "ingredients drive success". This is the most transferable lesson of the study.

**4. Pre-specified hypotheses.**
- Glycerin + sodium hyaluronate and glycerin + hyaluronic acid show no association with rating (adjusted q ≈ 0.9).
- Glycerin + sodium hyaluronate is **negatively** associated with popularity after adjustment (β ≈ −0.11 SD, p ≈ 0.001). This is **fragile**: the raw test is not significant after correction, and the association disappears when deduplication is turned off. Report it as a lead, not a finding.
- Niacinamide + zinc PCA could not be tested.

**5. Robustness** ([RESEARCH_ROBUSTNESS.md](RESEARCH_ROBUSTNESS.md); LightGBM; BH correction within each check).

| Check | What changes | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Ablation (27 comparisons) | Add each ingredient feature family to Model A on its own | 7 nominal p < 0.05, **0 after correction** (min q = 0.13) |
| Other categories (24) | Model A vs B in Makeup, Hair and Fragrance | 3 nominal, **0 after correction** (min q = 0.31) |
| Sensitivity (27) | Review threshold 10 or 50; no deduplication | 0 nominal, 0 corrected |

- **Ablation.** Individual ingredients and combinations lean positive, most clearly for Skincare rating (+0.06 R²) and recommendation rate. Functional-group shares lean *negative*: they account for 4 of the 7 nominal results, all of them losses, and are what drags the full Model B down.
- **Ingredients only.** With no metadata at all, ingredients predict well below Model A on every target. They carry some information about success, but less than metadata does, and largely the same information.
- **Fragrance.** This is where Model B gains most (rating +0.10 R², random CV). That is plausible, since a fragrance's ingredient list is essentially its scent profile, but it is not significant after correction.

**Answer to the research question.** In this dataset, ingredient composition adds no statistically significant predictive information about product success beyond brand, category, price and existing popularity. This holds across models, validation schemes, thresholds and deduplication choices. A small number of individual ingredients show robust, modest associations. The point estimates hint at a weak, category-specific signal (individual ingredients in Skincare, the scent profile in Fragrance) that a larger or better-measured study (for example, with concentrations) could test. Everything here is reported as association only.


## 10. Limitations

- **Observational data.** Success is shaped by marketing, distribution, shelf placement and brand history, none of which are in the dataset. The adjusted test removes brand-level effects but not product-level marketing.
- **One retailer, one snapshot.** Sephora products as listed at one point in time. Products that failed and were delisted are absent (survivorship bias).
- **Ingredient presence is not concentration.** List position is only a proxy. Below 1%, ingredients may be listed in any order.
- **Functional groups come from the curated knowledge layer**, which maps about 72% of ingredient occurrences. Unmapped ingredients contribute nothing to group shares.
- **Recommendation rate is Skincare-only** and covers 1,529 products.
- **Ratings are bounded and skewed** (most products score between 4 and 4.7), which limits how much any model can explain.
- **High SHAP importance is not added value.** Ingredient features can carry brand and category information the model would also get from metadata. Step 5's A-vs-B comparison is the test of added value; SHAP describes how the model uses its inputs.
